faq:
  - q: "Do affirmations actually work?"
    a: "Sometimes, and it depends on how you use them. Research on self-affirmation — reflecting on your core values — is fairly strong and links it to lower stress and less defensiveness. But simply repeating a flattering statement you do not believe can backfire, especially for people with low self-esteem. Believable, value-based affirmations paired with action tend to help; unbelievable ones tend not to."
  - q: "How do affirmations work in the brain?"
    a: "There is no magic switch. One small fMRI study (Cascio and colleagues, 2016) found that reflecting on personal values activated brain regions tied to self-processing and reward. The more grounded explanation is ordinary repetition and attention: affirmations rehearse a kinder, steadier line of self-talk and point your focus toward what matters, which can influence how you feel and act over time."
  - q: "Why do affirmations not work for some people?"
    a: "A 2009 study by Wood, Perunovic and Lee found that repeating a positive self-statement like 'I am a lovable person' made people with low self-esteem feel worse, not better — because the line clashed with what they actually believed. When the gap between the affirmation and your honest self-view is too wide, your mind argues back. A believable, laddered version ('I am learning to treat myself with kindness') closes that gap."
  - q: "How long does it take for affirmations to work?"
    a: "There is no fixed timeline, and anyone promising one is guessing. Affirmations are a practice, not a switch — most people notice shifts in self-talk over weeks of consistent, attentive use, not overnight. They work best as one small habit alongside real action toward what you want."
  - q: "Are affirmations just wishful thinking?"
    a: "They can be, if they are vague claims you repeat while changing nothing. Used well, they are closer to deliberate self-talk: choosing the tone you speak to yourself in, focusing on your values, and motivating the next small step. That is supported by psychology far more than the idea that words alone make things appear."
