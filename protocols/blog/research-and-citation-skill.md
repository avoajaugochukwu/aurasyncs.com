# Research & Citation — the discipline of primary sources

> "Studies show" is contraband. Every load-bearing claim cites a named source the reader can click through to verify.

The fastest way to fail Google's Helpful Content classifier — and to lose credibility with any reader who knows the topic — is to write paragraphs full of factual claims with no traceable origin. This skill is the discipline of doing it the other way.

For a positive-affirmations blog, the cardinal sins are **a fabricated science claim** (a made-up "studies show 90% of people…" statistic, an invented neuroscience mechanism, or a misattributed psychology finding), **a misquoted or mis-cited scripture** (a Bible verse with the wrong wording, wrong reference, or no translation named), **a harmful affirmation** (toxic-positivity / denial framing handed to someone in genuine distress, or a guaranteed-outcome money/healing promise), and **a poorly-formed affirmation** (one that isn't first-person, present-tense, and believable, so it doesn't actually function). Affirmation content on the open web is *saturated* with confidently wrong claims — fake statistics, invented "rewires your brain in 21 days" mechanisms, and copied-around misquoted verses. Repeating them destroys credibility faster than any other error. The fake-statistic problem is the single biggest trust failure in this niche — catch it first. Verifying every claim, naming every translation, and confirming every affirmation is well-formed before publishing is non-negotiable — see the verification gate below.

## Fabricated science, misquoted scripture, and irresponsible claims are the cardinal sins (the verification gate)

Before any post ships, every factual claim, every affirmation, every scripture quote, and every responsible-claims line must be verified. This is a hard publish blocker.

For every affirmation in a draft (the lines the reader is meant to repeat):

1. **Confirm it is well-formed.** A working affirmation is first-person, present-tense, positive (states what *is* wanted, not what is feared), and believable enough that the reader can say it without their mind rejecting it (see `affirmation-craft-skill.md`). "I am calm and capable" works; "I will not be anxious" plants the very word it's fighting. A list of malformed affirmations is broken content.
2. **Confirm it is not harmful.** No denial framing for someone in real distress ("I am not depressed," "my anxiety is gone forever"), no toxic positivity that tells a grieving or struggling reader their feelings are wrong, no guaranteed-outcome promise. Where the topic is clinical (anxiety, depression, grief, weight, money hardship), a light, non-alarmist note that affirmations *support* but do not *replace* professional care must be present.
3. **Don't invent certainty.** If a practice's effect depends on the person or the situation (belief level, consistency, whether it's paired with action or therapy), say so — don't assert a false-universal "this will fix it."

For every domain claim (the psychology/science of affirmations, a cited study, a neuroscience mechanism, a health or money statement):

1. **Find the authority** — peer-reviewed psychology, a reputable mental-health organization (APA, NIH/NIMH), a university psychology department (`.edu`), or the named researcher who established the finding (e.g. Claude Steele's self-affirmation theory). Cultural sources (Louise Hay, Law of Attraction teachers) may be *attributed* as the origin of a practice, but never cited as scientific proof.
2. **Confirm the claim and its conditions.** Many findings are condition-dependent (self-affirmation effects are strongest under threat to self-integrity; neuroplasticity is real but does not mean "21 days rewires your brain"); cite the condition, not a bare absolute.
3. **Classify the result:**
   - **Verified** — the affirmation is well-formed and non-harmful, or the fact traces to a real authority → publish with the claim and link.
   - **Range only** — sources give a spread or the effect is genuinely conditional → publish that explicitly ("research links self-affirmation to lower stress in some studies; it is a support, not a cure"), don't overstate it as settled.
   - **Unsourced** — no authority and not demonstrable → cut it or reframe it as practice/belief language ("many people find that…"), never as confirmed science.
   - **Fabricated / harmful** — a "statistic" or mechanism no authority supports, a misquoted verse, or an affirmation that denies real distress → publish blocker, full stop. Fix or cut.

For every scripture quote:

1. **Name the translation** (NIV, ESV, KJV, NLT…) — a verse without a translation named is unverifiable and not publishable.
2. **Quote it exactly** and confirm the **reference is correct** (book, chapter, verse) against a reputable Bible source (e.g. Bible Gateway, a recognized publisher's text).

Run this as the dedicated **accuracy & trust pass** (see `accuracy-and-trust-skill.md`). A draft with any fabricated statistic, unsourced mechanism, misquoted verse, harmful affirmation, or guaranteed-outcome promise does not ship.

## Responsible claims (a hard rule)

Affirmations are wellbeing-adjacent — they touch mental health, faith, and money, which makes this a YMYL space. The trust line is not negotiable:

- **Mental health is not medical advice.** Where a post covers a clinical topic (anxiety, depression, grief, panic, weight, addiction), include a light, non-alarmist note: *"Affirmations are a supportive practice, not a substitute for professional care. If you're struggling, a doctor or licensed therapist can help."* Never tell a reader in genuine distress that positive words alone will fix a clinical condition, and never use denial framing ("I am not anxious") that asks them to pretend their experience away.
- **Scripture is quoted exactly, with the translation named.** "Be still, and know that I am God" — name it (e.g. *Psalm 46:10, NIV*). A wrong reference, altered wording, or unnamed translation is a publish blocker. Don't bend a verse to fit a theme.
- **Manifestation and money are mindset practice, never a guaranteed outcome.** Frame abundance/Law-of-Attraction/manifestation affirmations as a way to shift mindset, focus, and behavior — *not* as a promise that repeating words will produce money, a job, or a relationship. No "say this and the universe will pay your rent." Attribute Law of Attraction / Louise Hay as cultural practices, not as scientifically proven mechanisms.
- **No fabricated studies or statistics.** Never write "studies show 90% of people…", "scientists proved affirmations rewire your brain in 21 days," or any number you cannot trace to a real, named, linkable source. This is the #1 trust failure in affirmation content — if a stat has no source, cut it or replace it with attributed, honest language.

This discipline travels with every clinical, faith, and money subject through the whole pipeline — it is checked in the keyword brief (`keyword-research-skill.md`), the trust gate (`accuracy-and-trust-skill.md`), and the pre-publish audit (`google-trust-audit-skill.md`).

## What counts as a primary source

**Primary sources** are the original location where a fact was established. For an affirmations blog, the primary source of a science claim is the peer-reviewed study, mental-health org, or named researcher who established it; the primary source of a scripture quote is a reputable Bible reference with the translation named; and a well-formed, non-harmful affirmation is judged against affirmation-craft principles, not "sourced" like a fact. The hierarchy:

### Tier 1 — Always cite when available

- Peer-reviewed psychology journals and the named researchers behind a finding (e.g. Claude Steele's self-affirmation theory; Cohen & Sherman's self-affirmation reviews) — for the science of *why* affirmations can help
- Reputable mental-health organizations — the American Psychological Association (APA), NIH / NIMH, and equivalent national bodies — for stress, anxiety, self-talk, and wellbeing claims
- University psychology / behavioral-science department pages (`.edu` / institutional) — primary teaching and research material
- Reputable Bible references that name the translation (Bible Gateway, recognized publishers of the NIV/ESV/KJV/NLT) — for scripture quotes and references

### Tier 2 — Acceptable for general-knowledge claims

- Recognized reference works and reputable health encyclopedias (e.g. Mayo Clinic, Cleveland Clinic, Harvard Health) for general wellbeing context like positive self-talk and stress management
- Major outlets reporting on a named, traceable study (NYT, BBC health desks) — but follow the link to the underlying study and cite that
- Established practitioner texts (Louise Hay, Law of Attraction authors) — cited as the **cultural origin** of a practice, **never** as scientific authority for an outcome claim
- Official documentation from a professional body (e.g. a chaplaincy or pastoral body for a faith-practice framing)

### Tier 3 — Acceptable only when Tiers 1-2 are unavailable

- Wikipedia — **with care.** Use it only as a starting point, then follow its citations to the primary study / mental-health org / Bible source and cite that instead. Never cite Wikipedia as the load-bearing source for a science claim or a scripture reference.
- Reputable wellbeing sites run by recognized institutions or named, credentialed psychologists / clinicians
- Encyclopedias (Britannica) for general background, not for load-bearing facts

### Tier 4 — Contraband

Never cite these as load-bearing sources:

- AI-generated content (ChatGPT summaries, etc.)
- Random "manifestation hub" aggregators, content-farm "50 affirmations" listicles, and SEO spam — these are the single biggest source of fabricated statistics ("studies show 90%…"), invented neuroscience ("rewires your brain in 21 days"), and copied-around misquoted verses, and never count as proof of a fact
- "Law of Attraction secrets" or "the science of manifestation" listicles on content-farm blogs that cite no real study
- Pinterest, Reddit, Quora, Medium posts (unless the author IS a recognized authority — e.g. a credentialed psychologist publishing original work — and the post is the original publication)
- Forum posts and unsourced "this stat is everywhere" claims
- Any "scientific study" that cannot be located, named, and linked — if you can't find the actual study, the statistic does not exist
- Old archives of dead sites unless preserved by Wayback Machine
- Marketing/coaching sales copy pretending to be science

## The attribution format

Every external claim takes one of two attribution shapes:

### Inline attribution (preferred for most claims)

> According to the [American Psychological Association](URL), positive self-talk is associated with better stress management and resilience.

The link goes on the source identifier, not on the fact. The reader can verify by clicking the underlined text.

### Quoted attribution (for direct quotes and scripture)

> "Be still, and know that I am God," reads [Psalm 46:10 (NIV)](URL).

> Self-affirmation theory holds that people are motivated to maintain "the integrity of the self," as [Claude Steele's original work](URL) describes.

Claim, source, role/context, publication, translation/edition/date if relevant, link. Anything less is contraband.

### What an attribution must contain

| Element | Required? | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Source identifier | Yes | "the American Psychological Association" / "Psalm 46:10" |
| Translation (scripture) | Yes, always | "NIV" / "ESV" / "KJV" |
| Date / edition | Yes if the fact can change | "APA, 2023" |
| Publication / institution | Yes | "APA" / "NIH" / Bible Gateway / the researcher's journal |
| Author / body name | Yes if a named expert or researcher | "from Claude Steele's self-affirmation theory" |
| Role / context | Yes if helpful | "in studies of self-integrity under threat" |
| Link to the source | Yes | `[anchor](URL)` |

## Fair-use quoting

Quoting from copyrighted source material is allowed under fair use, but the rules matter:

### Length limits

- **Up to ~90 words** for most cases — a short excerpt from a psychology paper or a mental-health page, used for commentary or analysis
- **Single short quotes** — a one- or two-line definition or a single Bible verse quoted with attribution and translation is squarely fair use; cite the original publication
- **Scripture** — quoting individual verses with the reference and translation named is standard practice; quote what the post needs and link to the full passage on a reputable Bible site
- **Longer excerpts** — quote sparingly and only as much as the commentary needs; for extended passages, quote a short excerpt and link out to the original

### Always include
- Quotation marks (or blockquote formatting)
- Attribution (institution / author / scripture reference + original publication)
- Translation named, for any scripture
- Link to the source
- Edition / revision date where it pins down the fact

### When in doubt, paraphrase

If a passage is genuinely needed and you're unsure if you're within fair use, paraphrase and link out. Paraphrase still requires attribution. (Scripture is the exception — quote the verse exactly; never paraphrase a verse and present it as the verse.)

## Outbound link discipline

Links are how trust is paid forward and inherited. Rules:

### Always link
- Every named source on first mention
- Every factual claim drawn from an authority (psychology, mental-health, neuroscience)
- Every cited study or statistic
- Every scripture quote (link to the verse on a reputable Bible site, translation named)
- Every expert / researcher / organization on first mention

### Anchor text rules
- ✅ **Descriptive:** "the [APA's guidance on positive self-talk](URL)"
- ❌ **"Click here":** "...as found [here](URL)"
- ✅ **The named source:** "[Claude Steele's self-affirmation research](URL) found..."
- ❌ **The URL itself:** "as discussed at https://..."

### Where to link
- Inline within the prose, not in a footnote stack at the bottom (modern web readers don't scan footnotes)
- The first time a source appears, with attribution
- On the relevant phrase, not on a generic word

### How to handle the link
- Same-tab by default (`target="_blank"` is a UX preference, not an SEO requirement; the project's layout decides)
- No `rel="nofollow"` on legitimate primary sources — that's a signal that you don't trust them, and you shouldn't be citing untrusted sources anyway
- `rel="nofollow"` only for sponsored links, paid placements, or untrusted user-generated content
- `rel="ugc"` for legitimate user-generated content links (forum posts, comments)
- `rel="sponsored"` for affiliate or paid links

### Internal links to sibling affirmation sets and guides

Link the matching affirmation collection or sibling guide on first natural mention — e.g. `[affirmations for anxiety](/blog/affirmations-for-anxiety)`, `[how affirmations actually work](/blog/how-affirmations-work)`. These cross-links are both the reader's next step and the post's topical-authority signal; add the partner slug to the related-posts metadata where the schema supports it.

## When sources contradict

Real research turns up disagreement. Don't paper over it. Handle it explicitly:

### Pattern 1 — Two reasonable sources disagree
> "The evidence is mixed: some self-affirmation studies show reduced stress and defensiveness, while others find the effect is small or situation-dependent — affirmations are a helpful support, not a guaranteed fix." — give the range and the condition, not one bare "proven" claim.

### Pattern 2 — One source is clearly more authoritative
> "Wellness blogs often claim affirmations 'rewire your brain in 21 days,' but [the APA](URL) describes positive self-talk as a gradual practice with no fixed timeline — use the careful framing, not the viral number."

Cite both, but note which is current/authoritative.

### Pattern 3 — Sources contradict on a load-bearing fact
Don't pick a side without justification. For a science claim where authorities genuinely differ, write the disagreement *into* the post rather than asserting a false certainty:

> "Research links self-affirmation to lower stress under threat ([study](URL)), but the size and durability of the effect vary by person and situation — treat affirmations as a supportive habit, not a cure."

This is a strong E-E-A-T signal — it shows you read enough to know the finding is conditional. For scripture, there is no "contradiction" to split: quote the verse exactly in the named translation; if two translations word it differently, name both translations.

## Quoting people who are not "experts"

Sometimes a useful quote comes from a non-expert: a reader, a parent, a member of a faith community, someone sharing their own affirmation practice. These quotes are legitimate but follow different rules:

- Don't promote them to authority. "A reader who practices morning affirmations told us…" not "A psychologist noted…" Never let an anecdote override an APA page or a peer-reviewed study, and never let a personal-success story imply a guaranteed outcome.
- Get their consent before using their name. If they want anonymity, give them a descriptive identifier ("a new mom who used birth affirmations during labor")
- Don't fabricate composite reader testimonials — Google's HCU treats this as scaled content abuse, and a fake "this changed my life" story is a trust violation in a YMYL niche.

If you're writing about something the *author personally experienced* (you actually practiced gratitude affirmations each morning and noticed your mood lift), that's a first-person experience marker, not a "quote." See `eeat-signals-skill.md`.

## The research log

Before drafting any post, the writer keeps a research log (in scratch space, not in the final output):

```
Topic: <target query>

Sources collected:
- [URL] — <org/researcher/Bible source> — <relevance>
- [URL] — <org/researcher> — <relevance>
...

Domain claims collected (with source status):
- "<science/health claim>" — <APA / NIH / study / .edu> — [URL] — <verified | range only | unsourced>
- "<claim>" — <source> — [URL] — <status>
...

Scripture collected (exact quote + translation + reference verified):
- "<verse text>" — <Book chapter:verse> — <translation, e.g. NIV> — [URL] — verified? <yes/no>
...

Affirmations collected (well-formed + non-harmful):
- "<affirmation>" — first-person present-tense positive & believable? <yes/no> — any denial/toxic-positivity/guaranteed-outcome risk? <yes/no>
...

Responsible-claims check:
- Clinical topic (anxiety/depression/grief/money hardship)? <yes/no> — mental-health support-not-replacement note present? <yes/no>
- Manifestation/money framed as mindset, not guaranteed outcome? <yes/no>

Open questions / contradictions:
- <claim> — sources disagree: [URL1] vs [URL2]
```

The log feeds the post's outbound links and ensures every claim in the post traces back to something verifiable (a study / mental-health org / .edu for science, a reputable Bible source for scripture) — and that every affirmation is well-formed and safe.

## The WebSearch grounding pass (writer instruction)

Before writing the draft, the writer runs WebSearch on:

1. The exact target query (e.g., "affirmations for anxiety", "do affirmations actually work")
2. The 2-3 most important domain facts/claims in the brief (the science mechanism, the cited study, the scripture reference)
3. "self-affirmation theory site:.edu", "positive self-talk APA", "affirmations research NIH" for each load-bearing science claim
4. "<verse reference> NIV" / "<verse> Bible Gateway" to confirm exact wording and reference for each scripture quote
5. The relevant framing where one exists ("self-affirmation theory Claude Steele", "neuroplasticity positive self-talk evidence")

Then collect ≥ 8 grounding bullets. Each bullet must include the URL. Use **WebFetch** to read the precise fact, study finding, or verse wording from the authoritative page.

If WebSearch can only find:
- Random manifestation aggregators, "50 affirmations" listicles, and content farms → the topic has no real factual spine; build the post on well-formed affirmations + an honest how-they-work framing, and do **not** import their fabricated stats
- AI-generated SERP entries quoting fake "studies show 90%…" numbers on the first page → the topic is contaminated; ignore the numbers and ground only on real authorities
- No mental-health org, peer-reviewed study, or reputable Bible source for the load-bearing claims → mark the brief NEEDS MORE RESEARCH and stop, or drop the unsupported claim and keep the post to honest practice language

## The fact-check pass (writer instruction)

After drafting, for every load-bearing claim — a psychology/science statement, a cited study or statistic, a neuroscience mechanism, a health note, or a scripture quote — verify it with **WebSearch + WebFetch** against an authority. Every domain claim also goes through the dedicated accuracy & trust pass (see `accuracy-and-trust-skill.md`); every affirmation is confirmed well-formed and non-harmful; every verse is confirmed exact with its translation named.

Procedure:

1. **WebSearch** for the fact/definition/verse at an authority, e.g. `self-affirmation theory definition APA`, `positive self-talk stress NIH`, `Philippians 4:13 NIV Bible Gateway`.
2. **WebFetch** the authoritative page and read the precise fact, finding, or verse wording.
3. **Patch the draft inline** if the authority contradicts or refines what you wrote. Patches are **literal swaps only** — never reorganize sections during this pass. If a statistic can't be sourced, **delete it** in the patch.

If the authority contradicts a claim that came directly from the brief / RESEARCH block, trust the RESEARCH (it is the curated brief) and note the disagreement at the end of the audit instead of patching — **except** for a fabricated statistic or a misquoted verse, which is always cut/corrected regardless of where it came from.

Skip the fact-check pass entirely if the brief carries an explicit "research is final, do not fact-check" instruction — but the scripture-accuracy and no-fabricated-stats checks are **never** skippable.

## Citation density by type

How many primary-source citations should appear in a post:

| Content type | Minimum primary citations |
|---|---|
| 📝 Pillar / big collection (e.g. "365 daily affirmations") | 4-8 |
| ✨ Themed affirmation collection ("Affirmations for X") | 2-5 (authority for any science/how-it-works claim; a mental-health note where clinical) |
| Practice guide (how affirmations work / write your own / manifestation) | 4-6 (study / mental-health org / .edu for each mechanism claim) |
| 📖 Faith / scripture set (Bible / Christian) | 2-5 (a reputable Bible source per verse block, translation named; +1 for any wellbeing claim) |
| 🌅 Daily / occasion set (morning, Monday, birth) | 1-3 (any factual or health context; a mental-health note where clinical) |

A post below the minimum should be flagged in the audit. For practice guides and any post asserting science, every domain claim that is not common knowledge needs a study / mental-health org / .edu source; every affirmation must be well-formed; every verse must be exact with its translation named.

## Citation pitfalls the audit catches

- **Fabricated statistic** ("studies show 90% of people who use affirmations…") with no traceable source → publish blocker; cut it or replace with attributed, honest language. This is the #1 failure mode in affirmation content.
- **Invented mechanism** ("affirmations rewire your brain in 21 days") → publish blocker; reframe to what a real authority actually says about neuroplasticity / self-talk, or cut
- **Misquoted or mis-cited scripture** (wrong wording, wrong reference, no translation named) → publish blocker; quote exactly, fix the reference, name the translation
- **Harmful affirmation** (denial framing for someone in distress, toxic positivity, guaranteed-outcome money/healing promise) → publish blocker; rewrite well-formed, add the mental-health support-not-replacement note
- **Claim sourced only to a manifestation blog or listicle** → trace to a study / mental-health org / .edu or downgrade to honest practice language ("many people find that…")
- **Wikipedia as the only source for a load-bearing claim** → fix by following the citation to the primary source
- **Claim without a link** → fix by linking
- **Dead links** → fix by replacing with archive.org or a fresh source
- **False certainty** (asserting a conditional research finding as settled fact) → present the range and the condition
- **Malformed affirmations** (future-tense "I will…", negation "I am not anxious", unbelievable leaps) → rewrite per `affirmation-craft-skill.md`
- **AI-summarized facts** (psychology or neuroscience from an LLM with no source) → rewrite from a verifiable authority
