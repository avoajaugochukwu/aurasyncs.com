# Accuracy & Trust — The Hard Gate

The internet is full of affirmation posts that cite a study that doesn't exist, promise that saying the right words will make money appear or anxiety vanish, misquote a Bible verse, or hand a person in real distress a phrase that tells them to deny how they feel. An affirmations site that ships any of those is just another thin wellness aggregator — and worse, it can leave a vulnerable reader feeling more alone when the magic words don't work. A site whose affirmations are genuinely well-formed and whose every claim is honest and sourced earns the trust, the links, and the rankings.

**The rule: no affirmation and no factual claim ships unless it is verified.** Affirmations are verified by checking they are well-formed and safe; facts, science, scripture, and health/money claims are verified against an authority. A confident-but-wrong claim — or a beautiful affirmation that quietly gaslights someone — is not a small error. It is the one thing this whole site exists to get right: a real person, often at a low moment, trusting us.

---

## Two kinds of content, two ways to verify

### 1. The affirmations themselves → they must be well-formed and safe

The first-person lines a reader will repeat. Following them should leave the reader *steadier*, not in denial.

- **Source of truth:** the affirmation-craft contract (`affirmation-craft-skill.md`). Each affirmation is present tense, first person, positively framed (affirm what you want, not what you fear), believable for where the reader starts, specific, and emotionally true.
- **Read each one as the reader.** Would saying this out loud help, or would it feel like a lie they have to swallow? An affirmation the reader can't believe yet should be laddered ("I am learning to…", "I am open to…"), not asserted as a flat fact they'll reject.
- **No toxic positivity or denial.** For clinical or grief-adjacent topics (anxiety, depression, illness, loss, weight), never write an affirmation that denies the feeling ("I am never anxious", "I feel no pain") or implies the reader's struggle is their fault. Affirm capacity and direction ("I am safe in this moment", "I am allowed to rest"), not erasure.
- **On-theme and accurate.** A "money affirmations" line is about the reader's relationship with money and action, not a spell. A chakra or scripture affirmation uses that tradition's language correctly.

### 2. Facts, science, scripture & health/money claims → an authority wins

Anything the post asserts as true: the psychology of why affirmations work, a cited study or statistic, a Bible verse and its reference, a health or financial claim.

- **Acceptable sources:** peer-reviewed psychology and reputable mental-health bodies (APA, NIH/NIMH, university psychology departments) for the science; a reputable Bible reference for scripture (and always name the translation); recognized practitioners named *as cultural sources, not scientific authority* (e.g. attributing a phrasing to Louise Hay or describing Law of Attraction as a belief system, not a proven mechanism). Wikipedia is a starting point, never a final citation — follow it to the study or the primary source and cite that.
- **Synthesize and attribute — never copy, never inflate.** "Self-affirmation theory, developed by psychologist Claude Steele, suggests that reflecting on your core values can reduce defensiveness and buffer stress" (cited) beats "science proves affirmations rewire your brain." State what the research *actually* supports, including its limits.
- **Where a claim genuinely varies or is uncertain, say so.** Much of the affirmation evidence base is modest or mixed — present it honestly as "may help", "has been linked to", not "is proven to."

### Trust rule: responsible claims (YMYL)

Affirmations sit next to mental health, faith, and money — Your Money or Your Life territory. False or over-promising claims here are a trust *and* a safety failure.

- **Support, not a substitute.** Affirmations are a supportive practice, never a replacement for medical, psychological, or financial care. Where the topic is clinical (anxiety, depression, grief, illness, weight, addiction), add a **light, non-alarmist note** pointing the reader to a professional if they're struggling. One warm sentence, not a scary disclaimer wall.
- **No guaranteed outcomes.** Never promise that affirmations will cure a condition, make money appear, heal an illness, or manifest a specific result on a timeline. Frame manifestation and money affirmations as **mindset and behavior** practices that can shift focus and motivation, not as cause-and-effect magic.
- **Scripture handled with care.** Quote verses exactly, reference them correctly (book chapter:verse), and name the translation. Don't present a paraphrase as a quote. Frame faith affirmations respectfully as devotional practice.
- **No fabricated statistics or studies.** This is the single most common failure in affirmation content. If you can't source a number or a "study" to a real authority, cut it. "Studies show 90% of people…" with no real citation is an automatic blocker.

## The verification ladder

Every load-bearing affirmation/claim gets one of four buckets:

| Bucket | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Verified | Affirmation is well-formed and safe; or the fact/scripture matches an authority | Ship it. Cite the authority for facts, studies, and scripture. |
| Range / hedged | The evidence is mixed or the value depends on context | Ship it **honestly hedged** ("may help", "has been linked to") and name the limit. Never inflate to certainty. |
| Contradicted | An affirmation is harmful/denying, or a claim contradicts the authority (misquoted verse, wrong study finding) | Correct it. Re-form the affirmation or fix the fact, then re-check around it. |
| Unverifiable / fabricated | An invented stat, a study that doesn't exist, a guaranteed-outcome promise, a harmful affirmation that can't be salvaged | Cut it or replace with a sourced/safe version. Do not ship. |

A post may ship with Verified and Range items. A post that still contains an unresolved Contradicted or Unverifiable item **does not ship**.

## The verification mechanism

### For affirmations — read them as the reader

1. **Read each affirmation aloud in your head** as the person who searched this topic at a low moment.
2. **Check the form:** present tense, first person, positive framing, believable (or laddered), specific, on-theme.
3. **Check the safety:** no denial of a real feeling, no blame, no toxic positivity, no implied guarantee. If it fails → Contradicted; re-form it (ladder it, soften it, make it affirm capacity) and re-read.

### For facts, science, scripture & claims — WebSearch / WebFetch an authority

There is no research API on this site. Use **WebSearch** to find an authority and **WebFetch** to read the exact fact, then check it. Batch independent lookups.

1. **WebSearch** for the claim at an authority, e.g. `self-affirmation theory Claude Steele evidence`, `do positive affirmations work research`, `Philippians 4:13 NIV exact wording`, `affirmations anxiety study APA`.
2. **WebFetch** the authoritative page (a peer-reviewed summary, a university/.gov page, a reputable Bible source) and read the precise fact, finding, or wording.
3. **Compare** with the draft:
   - Matches → Verified; add the citation.
   - Evidence is mixed / modest → Range; hedge honestly in the copy.
   - Contradicts the draft (wrong finding, misquoted verse) → Contradicted; correct it.
   - No credible authority (invented stat/study, guaranteed outcome) → Unverifiable; cut or replace.

For the affirmations themselves you don't need WebSearch — read them against the craft contract.

## High-risk claim types (verify with extra care)

- **"Studies show…" statistics** — the #1 failure in affirmation content. Never invent a number or a study. Source it to a real authority or cut it.
- **"How affirmations work" mechanisms** — self-affirmation theory, neuroplasticity, "rewiring your brain". State what the research actually supports and its limits; cite it. Don't overclaim a proven mechanism the evidence doesn't establish.
- **Scripture** — exact wording, correct reference, named translation. Never quote from memory; confirm against a reputable source.
- **Clinical topics** (anxiety, depression, grief, illness, weight, addiction) — affirmations must be supportive and non-denying, and a light support-not-replace note must be present.
- **Manifestation / money / Law of Attraction** — frame as mindset/behavior, never as guaranteed cause-and-effect. No "say this and money comes."
- **Health & healing affirmations** — never imply affirmations treat or cure a medical condition. Affirm capacity, calm, and care-seeking, not cure.

## How to present verified, hedged, and corrected content

**Verified (affirmation, well-formed and safe):**
> I am safe in this moment, and this feeling will pass. *(present tense, first person, affirms safety without denying the anxiety)*

**Verified (fact, checked against an authority):**
> Reflecting on your core values — what self-affirmation theory calls a "values affirmation" — has been shown in psychology research to reduce stress and defensiveness. *(per a university psychology source)*

**Range / hedged (evidence is modest):**
> Affirmations won't fix everything, but research suggests that regularly affirming what matters to you *may* lower stress and support a steadier mindset. *(honest hedge, not "proven to cure")*

**Corrected (contradicted the draft):**
> "I am never anxious" rewritten to **"I am learning to meet my anxiety with calm"** — the first denies a real feeling; the second affirms capacity and is believable.

**Trust (responsible framing):**
> Affirmations are a gentle, supportive practice. If anxiety is frequent or overwhelming, please reach out to a doctor or therapist — saying kind words to yourself works best alongside real support, not instead of it.

## Integration with the write/review pipeline

- **In the writer pass:** run this verification after drafting and before the re-audit. The audit's "Affirmations & facts verified (N)" block reports every item's bucket.
- **In the review pass:** affirmation/claim verification is part of the audit. Any Contradicted/Unverifiable left unresolved blocks the rewrite from overwriting the file.
- **Gate behavior:** if any load-bearing claim ends Unverifiable (a fabricated study, a guaranteed outcome) or any affirmation ends Contradicted-and-unsalvageable (harmful, denying), emit the audit with `POST NOT SHIPPED — claims unverified / affirmations unsafe` and do not write the file.
